# Understand the market

Use this to choose a direction or answer a consequential question about an
existing business. The result is a supported decision or a clear next question,
not a mandatory market-research dossier.

## Establish the question

Start with the founder's goal, expertise, customer access, existing assets and
constraints. With no idea yet, ask an easy question about a recurring problem,
experience or people they can reach; do not require a finished brief. If the
founder asked for no questions now, say where you will start and keep the
question for later. For an open-ended request, state useful assumptions and
compare plausible directions.
For an existing product, identify the decision at stake and reuse its evidence.

Choose the smallest useful investigation after reading the relevant project
materials, prior decisions and founder context. Establish what is already known,
which consequential gap remains and what decision its answer could change;
clarify missing context or use external sources only where that matters. For
substantial research, prepare `egbe-docs/research/<id>/brief.md` with the question,
relevant context and evidence, gaps, needed result and constraints such as market,
period or budget. Link sources instead of copying full reports. A narrow operation
can use a short assignment in the existing task or explicit inputs; no fixed form
is needed. Use search, scraping,
keyword data or `egbe-os-product-market-research` for the relevant question, not
every available research section. Existing evidence can be sufficient; a research
request does not itself authorize paid queries, contacting people or launching.

## Examine the relevant parts

Use these lenses as needed, not as required stages:

- **Customer and problem:** who experiences the problem, who buys, what triggers
  demand, current workarounds, urgency and switching constraints. Prefer actual
  customer language and behavior over invented demographic personas. A user,
  buyer and approver can be different people, not three separate segments.
- **Alternatives:** include direct products, adjacent solutions, manual work and
  doing nothing. Compare the same job, market and buying situation; popularity,
  funding or ad activity alone does not establish commercial success.
- **Offer and positioning:** explain a useful result and why someone would choose
  it over those alternatives. Ground differentiation in deliverable value, not
  a feature list or fashionable technology. Test founder assumptions as well as
  agent proposals; preserve core constraints rather than optimizing them away.
- **Demand:** distinguish stated interest, problem evidence, active searches,
  trial behavior and willingness to pay. Search volume, positive comments or a
  long-running competitor ad do not prove purchases. Investigate contrary
  evidence, not only examples supporting the idea.
- **Pricing and economics:** understand existing spending, who controls budget,
  charging units and delivery costs. Treat willingness to pay and retention as
  hypotheses unless observed. Use contribution after relevant variable costs,
  not revenue alone, when reasoning about affordable acquisition; avoid assumed
  lifetime value hiding an uneconomic first transaction.
- **Market and reach:** distinguish a broad market estimate from the audience
  this offer can actually reach and serve. Preserve geography, currency, period
  and units; avoid summing overlapping segments or searches. Show assumptions
  behind estimates rather than manufacturing precision or a fixed market score.
- **Acquisition evidence:** identify where customers seek solutions or can be
  reached, relevant costs and barriers. Inspect previous tests and comparable
  evidence; separate observed facts from forecasts. Select the next test with
  [choose-test](choose-test.md), not a channel ranking detached from the offer.

Use the technical research skill's source and calculation checks. Preserve
provenance, dates, coverage and uncertainty; distinguish first-hand observations
from inference. Repeated or syndicated sources are not independent corroboration.
Partial funnel access, stale receipts or missing data must limit the conclusion,
not be filled with imagined outcomes.

## Keep market evidence useful

For market findings that support an important decision, keep the original source,
observation date, applicability to this business, a justified next review point
and signals for an earlier check. Choose freshness according to decision
relevance, market change and collection cost, not the frequency of reports.
Reuse sufficient evidence; refresh a consequential gap, a due finding or an
observed change with a targeted question that could alter the decision. A new
day does not require repeating the market study.

## Recommend and hand off

Compare commercial plausibility, ability to deliver first value, customer access
and the largest remaining uncertainty. Account for money, founder time and
operating commitments; use Product's [offer method](../product/offer.md) where
these constrain the offer. Do not recommend building simply because research is complete.

Present the supported conclusion, meaningful alternatives, counterevidence and
what would change the recommendation. Keep new research in its existing research
folder. Keep the chosen product direction in the existing requirements or task
record: customer, problem, first useful result, basis, unresolved assumptions and
next decision. This product decision is not the research assignment's input brief.

For an acquisition question, carry findings into the current marketing plan and
[choose-test](choose-test.md). For agreed product definition, hand the selected
direction and evidence boundary to [Product](../product.md). Research may inform a PRD;
it need not generate one. Naming, public promises and subsequent implementation
remain separate founder decisions.
